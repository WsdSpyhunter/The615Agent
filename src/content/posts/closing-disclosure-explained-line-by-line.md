The Closing Disclosure is the document that shows what your mortgage and closing will actually look like. It can feel dense the first time you see it. Most of it makes sense once you know what to compare it against and which questions to ask.

This is a general walkthrough, not legal, tax or lending advice. Your lender and closing attorney or title company are the right people to confirm the details of your own loan.

## When the Closing Disclosure arrives

![Photo: Real estate, homeownership, homebuying, home, house](/img/blog/closing-disclosure-explained-line-by-line/inline-1.jpg)

*Photo by [OleksandrPidvalnyi](https://pixabay.com/users/OleksandrPidvalnyi-4638469/) on [Pixabay](https://pixabay.com)*

A lender must send the Closing Disclosure at least 3 business days before closing. Put that date on your calendar as soon as you have a closing date, because those 3 business days are your review window.

Don't save it for the night before. Read it the day it arrives, mark anything unclear, and send your questions to your lender right away. If something needs correcting, you want time to get it fixed.

I also suggest sharing it with your agent. A second set of eyes catches things, especially if you are reading a form like this for the first time.

## Start with the Loan Estimate

The best way to read a Closing Disclosure is next to your Loan Estimate. After you apply, a lender must give you a Loan Estimate within three business days. It is a standard three-page form, which is why estimates from different lenders can be compared side by side.

Put the two documents next to each other and go through them in order:

- The loan amount
- The interest rate
- The estimated monthly payment
- The lender fees and other closing costs
- The cash you need to bring to closing

Some differences are normal, since the Loan Estimate is an estimate. But every difference deserves a question. If a fee went up, ask what it is for and why it changed. If a fee you expected is missing, ask about that too. A short, polite email to your lender is enough.

The CFPB encourages borrowers to get Loan Estimates from more than one lender, because rates and fees can differ. The [CFPB Owning a Home guides](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/) explain these forms in plain language and are worth keeping open while you read.

## Check the loan terms and monthly payment

![Photo: Mortgage, house, contract, sign, home](/img/blog/closing-disclosure-explained-line-by-line/inline-2.jpg)

*Photo by [Tumisu](https://pixabay.com/users/Tumisu-148124/) on [Pixabay](https://pixabay.com)*

Start with the numbers that shape your budget for years.

**Interest rate.** Confirm it matches what you expected. For context, Freddie Mac via FRED reported the 30-year fixed mortgage rate at 7.18% for the week of 2026-11-05. Your own rate depends on your loan and your finances, so the number on your document may differ. What matters is that it matches what you and your lender agreed to.

**Loan type.** Make sure the loan is the one you chose. Conventional, FHA and VA loans work differently. FHA loans allow a down payment as low as 3.5 percent. VA loans typically require no down payment and no monthly mortgage insurance, though a funding fee may apply.

**Mortgage insurance.** Private mortgage insurance is typically required on conventional loans when the down payment is under 20 percent. If it appears in your payment, you should know why.

**Fixed or adjustable.** If you chose an adjustable-rate mortgage, the rate is fixed for an initial period and can then change. Check that the document reflects what you intended. The CFPB has plain-language guides on how ARMs work.

If you want to see how changes in rate or down payment affect the monthly figure, try the [mortgage calculator](/mortgage-calculator) before you talk to your lender.

## Understand the closing costs and cash to close

Closing costs typically run 2 to 4 percent of the purchase price, plus earnest money and moving costs. That is a rule of thumb, so your actual total depends on your loan and the transaction.

When you review the costs, sort them in your head into three groups:

- Fees charged by your lender for the loan
- Third-party services, such as title work, an appraisal or recording
- Prepaid items, like insurance and taxes set aside for the coming months

For each group, ask whether the amount matches the Loan Estimate and, if not, why. You are allowed to ask what any line is. A good lender will explain it without hesitation.

The cash to close figure is the one that matters most on the final day. Check it against your own bank balance, and remember that earnest money you already paid is typically accounted for. If the figure is higher than you planned, speak up now.

Taxes and school assignment depend on the exact address. If a tax figure looks off, verify it with the county. For Williamson County homes, the [Williamson County property search](https://www.williamsoncounty-tn.gov/2099/35739/Property-Data-Search) is a starting point. Spring Hill includes parts of both Williamson and Maury counties, so county services, schools and taxes can differ by address.

If you are working with a down payment or closing cost assistance program, ask your lender whether they take part in programs from the [Tennessee Housing Development Agency](https://www.thda.org), and check that any assistance shows up correctly.

## Protect your money and your loan before closing

Two habits matter between the Closing Disclosure and the closing table.

**Verify wire instructions by phone.** Wire fraud is real. Call a number you already trust, not one from an email, to confirm instructions before sending money. Never rely on emailed wire instructions, even if the message looks like it came from someone you know.

**Keep your finances steady.** Avoid opening new credit, financing furniture, or changing jobs from the time you apply through closing. A pre-approval letter is not a final loan commitment. Final approval depends on the appraisal, underwriting and your finances staying the same until closing.

HUD also has general information on the process in its [buying a home guidance](https://www.hud.gov/topics/buying_a_home). If you ever have concerns about how a licensed real estate professional has handled a transaction, the Tennessee Real Estate Commission is the state regulator.

## A short checklist for your review

1. Note the date you received it and confirm closing is at least 3 business days away.
2. Compare it with your Loan Estimate, item by item.
3. Confirm the loan type, rate and monthly payment.
4. Review the closing costs and cash to close.
5. Send written questions to your lender and keep the answers.
6. Verify wire instructions by phone.
