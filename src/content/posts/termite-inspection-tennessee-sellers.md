If you are selling a home in Tennessee, the termite inspection is one item that can catch you off guard. Termite and wood-destroying organism (WDO) inspections are commonly requested by buyers and lenders in Tennessee. This post covers what that means for you as a seller and what to sort out early.

I am not giving legal or pest control advice here. This is how the pieces fit together, so you can ask better questions of the professionals you hire.

## What a WDO inspection is

![Photo: Shutters, architecture, window, house, wooden](/img/blog/termite-inspection-tennessee-sellers/inline-1.jpg)

*Photo by [psaudio](https://pixabay.com/users/psaudio-834929/) on [Pixabay](https://pixabay.com)*

WDO stands for wood-destroying organism. Termites are the one most people think of. A termite or WDO inspection is a focused look at the home for signs of those pests and any related damage.

It is separate from the general home inspection. Radon testing, a sewer scope and a termite inspection are common add-ons to a standard home inspection, so a buyer may order several things at once. If radon comes up, the [EPA radon information](https://www.epa.gov/radon) page is a good official place to read about it.

Ask any company you hire what its report covers and how to read it. Do not assume every report looks the same.

## Who asks for it, and what to confirm

Buyers and lenders commonly request these inspections. That is the extent of what I can say as a general matter. How it plays out on your sale depends on the buyer, the loan and your contract.

A few things are worth confirming with your agent when you list:

- Who orders the inspection and who pays for it, as the contract spells out.
- What happens if the report shows something.
- Which dates in the contract apply to inspections and repair requests.

Read the contract language itself. A friend's experience or a general rule of thumb will not tell you what your own paperwork says.

## If the report finds something

![Photo: Control, work, clerk, form, business](/img/blog/termite-inspection-tennessee-sellers/inline-2.jpg)

*Photo by [jarmoluk](https://pixabay.com/users/jarmoluk-143740/) on [Pixabay](https://pixabay.com)*

Nobody knows what a report will show until the inspection is done. If it does show activity or damage, a repair request usually becomes part of the negotiation. What happens next depends on your contract, the buyer and the home.

A few general steps help:

1. Get the report in writing and read it fully before you respond.
2. Ask the company or a pest professional to explain what the findings mean and what treatment or repair would involve.
3. Talk with your agent about your options, which can include doing the work, getting quotes, or negotiating in another way.

I will not put a dollar figure on any of this. Costs depend on the home and the findings, and a written quote from a qualified professional is the only number worth planning around.

If your home is older, it can help to read up on what to watch for. The [Franklin older homes](/franklin/older-homes) page is a place to start, and the same questions apply to older homes in other towns.

## Disclosures and paperwork

A termite report does not replace the seller paperwork. Tennessee sellers complete the residential property condition disclosure (or an exemption). The lead-based paint disclosure applies to homes built before 1978.

Answer the disclosure forms accurately and completely. If you are unsure how a past treatment, repair or known issue should be handled on the form, ask your agent first. For a legal question, talk with a real estate attorney. For questions about how agents are regulated in Tennessee, the Tennessee Real Estate Commission is the official source.

If you are not sure how old your home is, you can look up the property record for your address on the [Williamson County property search](https://www.williamsoncounty-tn.gov/2099/35739/Property-Data-Search). Other counties have their own records, and Spring Hill, for example, includes parts of both Williamson and Maury counties, so details can differ by address.

## Where the market fits in

Local numbers do not decide whether you need an inspection. They do help you plan your timeline and your expectations.

Franklin, as of 2026-09-30 (Redfin, rolling 3 months), shows a median of 55 days on market, an average sale-to-list ratio of 98.1%, and 4.0 months of supply. Months of supply is how long it would take to sell every active listing at the current pace of sales. Under 4 months favors sellers, 4 to 6 months is balanced, and over 6 months favors buyers. At 4.0, Franklin sits right at the start of the balanced range.

For the wider area, Redfin reports the Nashville metro for the 4 weeks ending 2026-10-25 with a median of 67 days on market, a sale-to-list ratio of 97.6% and 5.7 months of supply.

These are area-wide figures and they do not predict what will happen with your home. Price range, condition and location all matter. You can see more figures on the [Market data for the I-65 corridor](/market-data) page.

## Questions to bring to your agent

Before you list, it helps to have a short list ready:

- What is the age of the home, and is there any record of past termite treatment or repair?
- Would it make sense to talk with a pest professional before listing, or to wait and see if a buyer asks?
- How should I answer the disclosure questions about anything I know?
- If a report comes back with findings, what are my options under my contract?

There is no single right answer to the second question. It depends on your home, your timeline and your comfort with surprises.
